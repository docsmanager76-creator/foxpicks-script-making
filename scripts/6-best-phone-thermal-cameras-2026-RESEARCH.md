## 3. Buyer checklist (spec-matrix columns)
1. Real (native) detector resolution vs the upscaled "super resolution" figure
2. Sensitivity (NETD, mK)
3. Accuracy claim and its conditions
4. Temperature range
5. Phone support: iPhone USB-C, Lightning adapter in the box, Android version limits
6. Power: from the phone (watts) or its own battery
7. Focus: fixed vs manual macro
8. Visible camera / image fusion
9. Ruggedness: drop rating, IP, case
10. Weight and size
11. App: free, no registration, reports, PC software
12. In the box and warranty

## 5. Hook — type 5, Absurd Number
- **Material.** A phone thermal listing says 240x180 "super resolution". Its own bullet says the sensor is 80x60. That is 4,800 real temperature points under a number that suggests 43,200. TechRadar's Mark Pickavance wrote that FLIR's cheaper camera "only has" 80x60 and that most marketing avoids saying so. PCWorld wrote that interpolation "does not produce any additional sensor detail".
- Same game on the 512x384 cameras: 196,608 on the box, 49,152 measured, a quarter.
- Hook names TechRadar and PCWorld (outlets), no brands from the six.
- **Open loops:** which camera is honest on the box (Klein prints 19,200), the villain, the free upgrade.

## 6. Myth — "the pixel number on the box tells you the camera"
- Same 256x192 sensor sits behind both the P3 and P4 (TechRadar, PCWorld); the dual lens, not pixels, is the P4's difference.
- The famous FLIR ONE Pro lists the loosest accuracy on the page (3 C or 5%, only at 15-35 C unit temperature) next to Thermal Master's 2 C and TOPDON's 2 C or 2% (maker claims, not tests).
- PCWorld's measured result is the only independent accuracy number: under 1.5 C against a contact thermometer on matte surfaces, off on bare metal. Tom's Guide snippet read about 3 C high. **These two disagree** (different samples and surfaces), so quote PCWorld attributed and do not rank accuracy.
- Repeatable line: "You're buying a sensor, not a number."

## 9. Free upgrade (~127-130 words)
**Set emissivity and shoot matte.** PCWorld: bare metal reflections distort readings; emissivity, distance and ambient temperature are adjustable in the app. Habit: put a strip of matte tape (masking or electrical) on shiny metal, set emissivity to match, then read the tape. Cross-source: all Thermal Master and TOPDON listings advertise adjustable emissivity; FLIR's table does not. Also a thermal camera "cannot see through" wood, concrete, water or glass (P4 listing note). Sources: PCWorld, listings. The previous handheld script also used electrical tape, so vary wording.
