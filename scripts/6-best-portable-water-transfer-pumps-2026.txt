##HOOK
Same pump, same tap water, same sales page. One line says ten gallons a minute. Another says four and a half. The only thing that changed is the height. Another pump prints two horsepower numbers on its own page, a half and a sixth. A third prints eighteen hundred gallons an hour in its bullets and thirteen hundred eighty in its spec table, and never names a height. Pro Tool Reviews gave one of these six to a service plumber, who used it flushing tankless water heaters. The rest comes from spec sheets and manuals read against each other. You will hear the one number that predicts your real flow. You will hear why the drill gadget on nearly every hardware store shelf is the one to skip. And there is a free hose trick that helps any pump. All the product links are in the description.

##P6
Number six, the TRUPOW PAS-30 1/10 HP 330 GPH Water Transfer Pump with 6 ft Hose Kit.

This is for draining a water heater, a clogged sink, or a flat roof, with an outlet in reach.

No outlet has tested it, so every number here is the maker's. It runs on a normal one hundred fifteen volt outlet, with a tenth horsepower copper motor. TRUPOW rates it at three hundred thirty gallons an hour, about five and a half gallons a minute. The listing gives a maximum lifting height of thirty nine feet. Hold that thirty nine feet, because a cordless pump several tiers above it on this list stops at fifteen. The discharge takes a standard three quarter inch garden hose. The kit includes a six foot stainless suction hose, and a strainer that drains water down to an eighth of an inch. It also ships with a spare impeller and spare carbon brushes, to stretch its service life. It weighs about five pounds, in stainless and carbon steel.

Now, the honest limits. It sits outside the water, so clear the air from the suction hose first. The maker publishes no dry run protection, so stay with it while it runs. And TRUPOW publishes no warranty term.

Its place in the lineup is the entry pick.

Buy it for small, occasional jobs. Skip it if you need to move a pool's worth of water.

##P5
Number five, the Superior Pump 91250 1/4 HP 1800 GPH Submersible Utility Pump.

This is for a flooded basement, window well, or pool cover, where the pump sits in the water.

No outlet has tested it, and Superior's own numbers disagree. The bullets say eighteen hundred gallons an hour, but the spec table on the same page says thirteen hundred eighty, and neither names a height. Here is the myth. The big number on the box is flow with almost no lift. Every foot you push the water up costs you flow. You're buying flow at your height, not the number on the box. Superior says it pushes water up twenty five feet. It runs on a quarter horsepower motor and a ten foot cord, and never needs priming. The outlet is inch and a quarter pipe thread, with a three quarter inch garden hose adapter in the box. It passes eighth inch solids, and the suction screen comes off for cleaning. The base fits a six inch floor drain hub. It weighs about seven pounds and is U L and C S A listed.

Now, the honest limits. There is no float and no auto shutoff. When the water is gone, you unplug it, or it runs dry. The warranty is one year.

Its place in the lineup is the value submersible.

Buy it for standing water you can reach. Skip it for a job you cannot watch.

##P4
Number four, the Milwaukee 2579-20 M12 Stick Transfer Pump.

This is for a plumber or homeowner pumping out a water meter box, a catch basin, a trench, or a sump pit.

No outlet has tested it yet, so these are Milwaukee's numbers. It is a long, thin pump. The aluminum barrel has a powder coated finish, and it goes straight down into the hole. A three hundred sixty degree filter at the bottom pulls water from every side. Milwaukee rates it at nine gallons a minute, and up to two hundred seventy five gallons on one XC four point oh battery. There is no suction hose, and nothing to prime. The outlet is a three quarter inch male pipe thread, so a garden hose adapter does the rest. It weighs under four and a half pounds. It runs on the same twelve volt batteries as Milwaukee's small drills and drivers.

Now, the honest limits. Remember the thirty nine feet at number six? This one lifts water only fifteen feet. That is plenty to clear a meter box into the yard. It is not enough to push water up a basement stair and out a window. And the 2579-20 is the bare tool, so the battery and charger are not in the box.

Its place in the lineup is the specialist pick.

Buy it for pits, boxes, and trenches. Skip it if your water has to climb.

##VILLAIN
Before the top three, the promise I made. The Milescraft DrillPump750.

It deserves a fair hearing. SlashGear argues a drill pump beats a dedicated tool for occasional jobs. The Milescraft sits on shelves at Woodcraft, Grizzly, Ace, and Canadian Tire. It primes itself, takes a garden hose, and carries a two year warranty, and you already own the drill.

Then read the fine print. Its seven hundred fifty gallons an hour needs a drill spinning about twenty eight hundred R P M, and its own listing table says three hundred. It needs silicone grease after every use, and it is not for drinking water. One owner took over half an hour to drain a fifty gallon water heater. Picture holding a drill trigger that whole time.

A pump that borrows your drill is a drill that stops being a drill.

Number six does that job with no drill. Buy this only for one small drain a year, or don't buy it.

##P3
Number three, the Wayne PC4 1/2 HP Cast Iron Transfer Pump.

This is for big jobs: a pool cover, a hot tub, or a long hose run.

This is the pump with two horsepower numbers, and no outlet has tested it. But Wayne's manual has something no other pick publishes, a real flow chart. Here the myth pays off. The chart starts at sixteen hundred gallons an hour with ten feet of lift. At forty feet, twelve hundred forty. At eighty feet, it still gives six hundred sixty, about eleven gallons a minute. A half horsepower brush motor sits in cast iron, with a cast iron pump body and thermoplastic impeller. Both ports are three quarter inch brass hose fittings, plus a suction strainer. The listing says it can boost a weak water line by up to forty pounds. It takes clear water from forty to one hundred twenty degrees. It weighs fourteen pounds, is assembled in the U S A, and needs a fifteen amp circuit.

Now, the honest limits. It does not prime itself, so fill the priming port first, and past ten feet of suction you need a foot valve. Running dry damages the shaft seal. It is not built for long, continuous runs. The warranty is one year.

Its place in the lineup is the heavy duty corded pick.

Buy it for big, planned jobs. Skip it if you want to plug in and walk away.

##P1

##FREE
Here is the free upgrade, and it works with any pump. Every pump pushes far better than it pulls. Number one lifts water eighteen feet on its suction side, but pushes it seventy five feet on the other. Wayne's manual calls for a foot valve once the suction side passes ten feet. Honda says its gas pump takes almost two minutes to prime at sixteen feet of lift. So set the pump as close to the water as the cord or battery allows. Keep the suction hose short, and full of water. Let the discharge hose cover the distance. And if your pump has no auto shutoff, stay with it until the water is gone. That habit costs nothing, and it decides whether you replace a pump or keep one.

##TAIL
Before you pay, ask one question. How high does the water have to climb? That one height decides your real flow, and almost no product page prints flow at height.

On the TRUPOW, confirm the hose kit. For a pond or a flooded yard, a two inch gas pump like the Honda WB20 moves far more, but weighs over forty pounds.
