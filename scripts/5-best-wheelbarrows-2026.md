##HOOK
Bob Vila's best overall wheelbarrow has four wheels. Not one of This Old House's five picks has a single wheel. Forbes Vetted put a one-wheel steel tub first. Three outlets, three answers.

The listings make it worse. Every one leads with cubic feet, a number that says little about what you can carry.

So I sorted five wheelbarrows and carts by how they handle weight, then checked every number against the listing I'm linking. I'll tell you which is number one and why.

Before that, a cart three outlets recommend and none can agree on. Then a free habit that makes any of these feel lighter. Hold that number, cubic feet, because one pick breaks it. All the product links are in the description.

##P5
Number five, the Best Choice Products 5-Cubic-Foot Dual-Wheel Yard Wheelbarrow.

This is for someone with a small yard who needs a light hauler a few times a month.

Bob Vila named it the best bang for the buck. Its testers ran it over a terrain course of soil and stone, and it handled a 30-degree slope well. They also liked the low-profile bin, which makes loading easy, and the foam grip.

The listing gives a five cubic foot plastic bin on a powder-coated steel frame, with two inflatable 14-inch rubber wheels. Air in a big wheel cushions every root and rut, so your wrists take less of the bumps. Hold that number, fourteen inches, because a pick near the top rolls on something very different. The padded handlebar doubles as a stand, so the barrow parks upright in a corner of the shed. The listing states a 330 pound weight capacity.

Compared with number four, you get a bigger bin and air in the tires, and you give up the hand truck tricks.

Now, the honest limits. Those same testers wrote that it looks and feels weaker than it actually is, that assembly is difficult, and that the hollow steel axle may weaken over time. That axle is the one to watch if you haul bricks every weekend. It matters much less if your loads are mulch and potting soil.

Its place in the lineup is the entry pick. I found no warranty term in the listing bullets.

Buy it for light, occasional yard work. Do not buy it if your weekends are stone and concrete.

##P4
Number four, the Worx WG050 Aerocart 8-in-1 Wheelbarrow and Hand Truck.

This is for someone who wants one tool that works as a wheelbarrow, a dolly and a hauler.

Three outlets list it. Bob Vila gave it Most Versatile and said it was the easiest to assemble of everything it tested. Family Handyman and This Old House made room for it too.

The listing says it carries up to 300 pounds, and that as a dolly it lifts 200. Worx claims that feels like lifting 17. That is the maker's claim, not a measurement. It has two wheels with dual support legs, and the listing says it converts into eight tools, with some accessories sold separately. Family Handyman says the design automatically centers the load, which keeps weight over the axle and out of your arms.

Now the myth this category runs on. Cubic feet. That test lists this cart at three cubic feet and 300 pounds. Number five has a bin two thirds bigger and a limit only thirty pounds higher. Reviewed shows the trick on its True Temper entry, six cubic feet heaped but three cubic feet flat. A cubic foot measures the tub, not the load. You're buying a weight limit, not a tub size.

Now, the honest limits. The hard wheels do not absorb impact, and the bin is smaller than the other carts in that test. It also has many moving parts to store. That hurts on rough ground and in a small shed. It does not matter on a paved patio.

Its place in the lineup is the specialist pick. This Old House lists a three year warranty; the bullets I read state none, so confirm it.

Buy it for many small jobs. Do not buy it for loose mulch by the truckload.

##VILLAIN
Before the top three, the promise I made. The Rubbermaid Commercial Products Big-Wheel Yard Cart, 8.75 cubic feet.

Bob Vila names its yard cart Best for Gardening and calls its volume the largest tested, though that was the 7.5 cubic foot version. Reviewed names this one Best Plastic and found it stable on gravel and sand. On paper it is clever. Flat-free 20-inch wheels, a rust-free tray, made in Virginia, and a bin that swallows a mountain of mulch.

Now read the limit. The listing says 300 pounds. Reviewed prints 200. Savvy Gardening prints twelve hundred. Three numbers for one cart. And 300 pounds over 8.75 cubic feet is about 34 pounds a cubic foot. Number three carries about 170.

A cart that can't tell you what it carries is a big bucket with extra steps.

For anything heavier than leaves, buy number two. Buy this one only for mulch, or don't buy it.

##P3
Number three, the Gorilla Carts 7-Cubic-Foot 1,200-Pound Flat-Free Poly Dump Cart.

This is for a big yard where you move soil, gravel and mulch by the load.

Bob Vila named the 1,200 pound Gorilla its pick for heavy loads, and Popular Mechanics also picks a 1,200 pound Gorilla dump cart for heavy loads. One catch. That test used the version with inflatable tires. This listing is the flat-free one, so comments about the tires do not carry over.

The listing gives a 1,200 pound capacity and a 39.5 by 27 inch bed. The tires are 13-inch and maintenance-free, so there is no pump and no puncture. A patented quick-release lever dumps the load. The listing calls the steering tight-turning, which is the maker's phrase, not a measurement.

Next to number four, it carries four times the weight. Next to number one, it carries twice as much, in a bed of seven cubic feet against four.

Now, the honest limits. That team found reverse steering difficult while towing, the high bed takes more effort to load, and it needs a big storage space. Hold that steering problem, because number two is built on one wheel.

Its place in the lineup is the large pick. The listing states a one year warranty.

Buy it for big loads in a big yard. Do not buy it for tight garden beds.

##P2
Number two, the True Temper 6-Cubic-Foot Steel Wheelbarrow with Never-Flat Tire.

This is for a homeowner who wants the classic one-wheel barrow for stone, soil and wet loads.

Three outlets agree. Reviewed named it Best Overall after an obstacle course. Forbes Vetted names the True Temper 6 cubic foot barrow its top pick, and Savvy Gardening's team made it the top pick too.

The listing gives a seamless steel tray with a sloped, curved front, which means wet soil slides out instead of clinging. It has steel handles with comfort grips and a heavy-duty undercarriage. The tire is a flat-free 4 by 8 inch design. Remember number five, fourteen inches of air. This tire has no air at all, so it never goes flat, but there is no cushion either. The testers who rated number four wrote the same about its hard wheels. Single tire design, the listing says, for balanced movement on any surface. Savvy Gardening calls it easy to maneuver, which fixes the steering problem on number three.

So why isn't it number one? Because it is heavy, and one wheel asks you to balance the load. Reviewed puts it around 46 pounds empty. Number one stays upright by itself.

Now, the honest limits. Reviewed saw the paint chip during its brick and shovel testing. Bare steel rusts, and Savvy Gardening flags the same weakness. It matters if the barrow lives outside. It does not matter if it hangs in a shed.

Its place in the lineup is the premium pick. I found no warranty term in the listing bullets.

Buy it for heavy, wet work. Do not buy it if one wheel makes you nervous.

Its place in the lineup is the one the other four get measured against. The listing states no warranty term, so lean on the one This Old House reports.

Buy it for mulch, soil, bricks and firewood. Do not buy it if you mix concrete in the tray.

That's why it's number one.

##FREE
Here's the free upgrade, and it works with every barrow and cart here. Put the heavy load over the wheel. Family Handyman says the Worx design automatically centers the load, and Worx claims 200 pounds can feel like 17. That is the maker's claim, but the lever is real. The closer a load sits to the axle, the less of it lands in your arms. So pack bricks and wet soil forward, over the wheel. Put the light stuff at the back. Then tip the tub toward the wheel when you dump. That habit costs nothing, and it decides whether a job leaves your back fine or sore.

##TAIL
Before you pay, ask one question. What is the weight limit in pounds, and are those cubic feet heaped or level? No product page tells you how it was counted.

Every pick is linked below, along with the Rubbermaid, and each had a live listing the day I checked. The Gorilla at number three is the flat-free version. Several favorites of other outlets, the Jackson and the Kobalt, did not turn up in my search, so I could not link them.

Quick recap. Light yard jobs, the Best Choice. Many small jobs from one tool, the Worx. Big loads in a big yard, the Gorilla 1,200 pound. Heavy, wet work, the True Temper. Everyone else, the Gorilla 600 pound.
