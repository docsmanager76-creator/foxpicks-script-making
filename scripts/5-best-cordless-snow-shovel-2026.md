##P5
Number five, the VOLTASK twenty-four volt, thirteen inch.

This is the first powered shovel for somebody who has only ever owned a plastic one. It is the lightest machine here, listed at nine and a third pounds, and that matters more than the spec sheet suggests. A snow shovel you dread carrying is one you leave in the garage.

Family Handyman ran the twelve inch version through four separate storms and made it their lightweight pick. Their sample weighed eleven pounds with the battery in, and the tester wrote that she loved everything about it. Her measurements are the useful part. Soupy, half-melted snow landed a few feet away. Packed snow flew about ten feet. Dry powder went eighteen. She cut through a two foot snowbank in several passes rather than one.

The feature to hold on to is the dial. This one turns the throw thirty degrees left or right, so in a tight walkway or a crosswind you decide where the snow lands, not the machine. Hold that, because number four does not have it, and the tester who liked that machine most called it its one real flaw.

Now, the honest limits. The scraper leaves a film of snow on the pavement, which Family Handyman flagged directly. And twenty-four volts is the lowest power here, in a category where Bob Vila's testers found higher voltage machines pulled ahead as the snow got wetter.

Battery and charger are in the box, and VOLTASK's own listing puts maximum runtime at twenty-five minutes, which is a refreshingly honest number. Buy this for a walkway, a deck and a short driveway. Skip it if you are regularly clearing six inches of wet snow.
##P4
Number four, the Wild Badger Power forty volt, twelve inch.

Now, the myth I promised, and it is the number you shop on. Runtime and throwing distance, as printed on the box, are close to fiction. Bob Vila's team measured nine machines and found the claims overshot reality by fifty to a hundred percent. One shovel promised sixty minutes and delivered twenty to thirty, and promised a twenty-five foot throw but managed fifteen.

Here is the honest half, because it is not every brand. This one claims about forty minutes, and Tom's Guide said that figure seemed accurate. Twenty minutes of work took two bars off a four bar gauge. The rule is not that manufacturers lie. The number was measured in dry powder, and that is not what is on your driveway at seven in the morning.

What he actually got is the useful part. A thirty by eight foot driveway cleared in about fifteen minutes. In two to three inches of wet snow it threw hard enough that he waited for cars to pass. Six inches of powder went through with ease, though the battery drained faster.

Now the honest limits. When the snow refroze overnight into two or three inches of icy crust, he gave up and went back to a manual shovel. And here is the thing I asked you to hold: it cannot aim. That was his one criticism, and the maker does not hide it. The listing itself calls the throw fixed and forward.

Eight inches of intake depth is the deepest bite here, battery and fast charger are included, and it carries a three year residential warranty. So why isn't it higher? Because it throws where it is pointed and nowhere else.

##FREEUP
Now the part nobody mentions.

Every team reached the same finding: these machines are very good on fresh snow and poor on snow that has refrozen. One reviewer cleared six inches of powder easily, then met three inches of icy crust next morning and reached for a hand shovel.

So change when you go out, not what you buy. Clear during the storm, or as soon as it stops, while the snow is loose. A second pass mid-storm beats one pass over ice.

And the scraper bar along the bottom is a wear part, sold on its own. When it thins, the machine stops touching the ground and leaves the film everyone complains about.

That habit costs nothing, and it decides whether this feels like a miracle or a disappointment.
