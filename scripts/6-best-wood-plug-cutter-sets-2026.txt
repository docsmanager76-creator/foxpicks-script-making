##HOOK
Three plug cutter sets, all with a half inch cutter, and they do not agree on what half an inch means. One set's own instructions tell you to fill the middle size hole with the plug from the biggest cutter. Another prints half inch on a cutter it also calls thirteen millimeters, which is wider than half an inch. And on Fine Woodworking's forum, a woodworker's cheap half inch cutter turned out plugs that came up short. The size stamped on the cutter is the number every box shouts. It is also the least reliable number on the box. You will hear why the label is not the plug you get. You will meet a hardware store favorite whose labels need a conversion chart. And there is a free habit that makes any plug vanish into the grain. All the product links are in the description.

##P6
Number six, the Rocaris R-294 8-Piece 1/4-Inch Hex Shank Wood Plug Cutter Set.

This is for someone hiding screw heads in a first furniture build, before buying a drill press.

You get eight cutters in four sizes: a quarter inch, three eighths, half inch, and five eighths. Each size comes twice, once as a straight cutter and once as a tapered one. Hold that number, because a set several tiers above it gives you three cutters in one style, and it still beats this one. Straight plugs have parallel sides, so they double as short dowels and patches over knots. Tapered plugs wedge into the hole like a cork, which closes small gaps around the screw head. Every cutter sits on a quarter inch hex shank. That snaps into a quick change chuck, and it will not spin loose in a regular drill chuck. Rocaris lists carbon steel with a black oxide finish, and says the set fits most power drills and hand tools.

Now, the honest limits. No major outlet has tested this set, so everything here comes from the maker's own listing. Like most plug cutters, these have no centering pin, so by hand they can wander as they start.

Its place in the lineup is the entry pick, and the maker does not publish a warranty.

Buy it to learn plugging on a starter budget, and skip it if you need anything past five eighths.

##VILLAIN
Before the top three, the promise I made. The Milescraft 5340 3-Piece PlugCutter Set.

On paper, it deserves a look. Milescraft fills hardware store aisles, and Journeyman HQ named a Milescraft cutter its budget pick. The set cuts tapered plugs for a snug fit. And Milescraft says the plugs eject on their own when you drill through three quarter inch boards. That is a clever set.

Then read its own instructions. The listing says the largest bit's plug fills the middle hole, and the middle bit's plug fills the smallest. So the labels do not match the holes. Drill a half inch counterbore, and nothing in this set plugs it.

A plug cutter that needs a conversion chart is a puzzle with extra steps.

For tapered plugs that fit, look at number two. Buy the Milescraft only if your counterbores stay at three eighths or smaller, or don't buy it.

##FREE
Here is the free upgrade, and it is about grain, not steel.

The enemy is a plug that shows. Wrong grain direction turns a hidden screw into a polka dot. So cut your plugs from an offcut of the same board. Before you free them, mark a small arrow on each top along the grain. When you glue, line the arrow up with the board. Spin the cutter before it touches wood, and keep the speed low. Plunge, then lift quickly, so no black burn ring forms. To free a whole row, cover the tops with masking tape and make one bandsaw cut along the bottom. The plugs come off on the tape. That trick comes from Fine Woodworking's forum.

That habit costs nothing, and it decides whether your plugs disappear or show up.
