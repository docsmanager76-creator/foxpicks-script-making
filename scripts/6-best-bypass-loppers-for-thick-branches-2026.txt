##HOOK
Almost every lopper box says heavy duty, built for thick branches. The claimed capacities run from one and three-quarter inches all the way up to three. So I checked what the tests actually found. Bob Vila tested a bypass lopper sold for three-inch branches. Its testers cut a two-inch green branch with ease, and the same size in oak took more effort. Outdoor Life's test wood topped out at two and a quarter inches. So on a bypass lopper, thick really means about two inches of live wood, not three. Bigger than that, and you want a saw. I read the Outdoor Life and Bob Vila tests, checked HGTV's picks, then went through every listing. Six loppers made the list, I will warn you off one more, and number one comes last. All the links are in the description.

##P6
Number six, the Fiskars 391561-1002 30-Inch Forged Bypass Lopper, 2-Inch Cut Capacity.

This is for someone who wants a simple, all-steel lopper with no gears or linkages to wear out.

The listing says it cuts branches up to two inches across. The thirty-inch handles supply the leverage, with no gear or compound linkage, so all the force comes from your arms. The blade is precision-ground stainless steel, fully hardened to hold an edge, with a low-friction coating that resists rust, sticking, and sap buildup. Construction is all steel, and Softgrip pads on the handles give a textured, non-slip hold. The blade is replaceable, so a worn or chipped edge does not retire the whole tool. It weighs three and a half pounds, lighter than the two Coronas and the PowerGear2 here. Fiskars lists a lifetime warranty.

I found no outlet that tested this model, so everything here comes from the listing.

Now, the honest limits. A plain bypass with no gearing asks more of your arms near that two-inch limit than the geared lopper at number two. The listing does not mention bumpers, so expect more shock at the end of each cut. And with no outlet test, I cannot tell you how it handles hardwood.

Its place in the lineup is the all-steel pick.

Buy it if you want simple and replaceable. Do not buy it if your branches are always near two inches.

Picture extending the handles for a high limb, then trusting a plastic lever at full reach.

Reach is only worth it if the tool holds together.

If you want reach, buy number three. For thick branches, buy number one. Buy this one only if you need telescoping handles.

##FREE
Here is the free upgrade, and it works with any lopper on this list.

These tips come from maker guidance and common practice, not a lab test.

First, use a bypass lopper on live, green wood. For dead, dry wood, reach for an anvil lopper or a saw.

Second, set the branch deep in the jaws, close to the pivot, where the leverage is strongest.

Third, keep the cutting blade on the trunk side, and cut just outside the branch collar.

Fourth, wipe off sap after each job, then oil the pivot and work the handles a few times.

Fifth, sharpen only the beveled side of the blade.

Sixth, if a branch is thicker than the listed cut capacity, use a pruning saw, because forcing it can spring the blades.

##TAIL
Before you pay, ask one question: does this claim come from a test or from a label? Heavy duty and thick branches are labels. Two inches of green wood with ease, and more effort in oak, came from a test. So did no strain at two and a quarter inches.

All seven links are in the description, including the WOLF-Garten. Check your thickest branch, live or dead wood, how far you reach, and how much weight you can hold overhead. Then keep a pruning saw for anything bigger.

Here is the quick recap. The Fiskars Forged is the all-steel pick. The Tabor is the compound-action pick. The Corona DualLINK is the bumper pick. The Hickok is the reach pick. The Fiskars PowerGear2 is the geared-power pick. And for most people, the Corona X Series Pro.
