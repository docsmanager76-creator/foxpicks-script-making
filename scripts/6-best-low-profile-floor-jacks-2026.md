##HOOK
Automoblog put floor jacks under real test vehicles. One of them starts three and a half inches off the floor. Another starts at five and a half, and it named that one its best floor jack. Two inches decides whether a jack goes under your car at all.

Now look at the listings. One jack here prints three different starting heights on a single page. Another never prints one. Every title says low profile.

So I sorted six jacks by that number, then by weight, reach and what each maker puts in writing. I will tell you which is number one and why. Before that, a jack that tops the roundups and may not fit under your car. Then a free habit that makes every jack safer. All the product links are in the description.

##P6
Number six, the DNA Motoring TOOLS-00234 2-Ton Low Profile Hydraulic Trolley Floor Jack.

This is for a regular car in a home garage, where you want a basic jack and nothing more.

I found no independent test of this exact model, so everything here is the maker's own listing. It states a two ton, four thousand pound capacity and alloy steel construction. The lifting range is five point one to thirteen inches, and that agrees in the title, the bullets and the spec table. Hold that number, five point one. It has an overload release valve, swivel casters and a large saddle. The listing states no warranty term. It tells you to contact the seller.

Now, the honest limits. Five point one inches is the highest start of any pick that prints one, and thirteen inches is the shortest reach in this video. The listing itself says it is not recommended for pickups or large SUVs. Automoblog's two ton Pro-Lift F-767 starts at three and a half inches. This one does not get near that.

Its place in the lineup is the entry pick, for ordinary ride heights. Its own bullet says the jack only lifts, and a jack stand must hold the load.

Buy it for a stock sedan. Do not buy it for a lowered car or a truck.

##P5
Number five, the Maxority 3-Ton Low Profile Hydraulic Steel Floor Jack.

This is for someone who wants more capacity than the entry pick and will measure the car before buying.

I found no independent test of this model either. The listing states a three ton, six thousand six hundred pound capacity, steel construction, and a dual piston rapid pump that needs fewer strokes. Maximum lift is nineteen point nine inches. The saddle has a built-in overload valve. The listing says ultra-low profile and extended reach. It never prints a starting height. Not in the title, not in the bullets, not in the spec table.

That is the myth in this category. Buyers shop by tonnage and by the words low profile. Neither predicts clearance. Number six holds two tons and starts at five point one. Number one holds two and a half and starts at three point three. Automoblog lists a three and a half ton jack that starts at five and a half, and a two ton jack that starts at three and a half. The Drive says its Best Overall four ton Big Red starts taller than most, while its Husky three ton starts among the lowest. You're buying a tonnage number, not clearance.

Now, the honest limits. No starting height, and the spec table states a ninety day warranty, the shortest term any pick here states.

Its place in the lineup is the value pick with real reach.

Buy it only after you measure. Do not buy it for a low car on faith.

##P4
Number four, the Jack Boss 3-Ton Aluminum and Steel Low Profile Racing Floor Jack.

This is for someone who moves the jack around, to a driveway, a track or a friend's garage.

Pro Tool Reviews spoke with a Sunex Tools veteran in a 2019 buying guide. His advice: aluminum jacks suit people who carry them, steel lasts longer but weighs more. That guide is not a test of this jack. The listing states three tons, an aluminum body on a welded steel frame, and a lifting range of three and fifteen-sixteenths to eighteen and five-sixteenths inches. The spec table agrees, at ten centimeters. It lists fifty-eight and a half pounds. The maker says it was tested to one and a half times its rating and meets ANSI and ASME standards. That is the maker's claim, not a lab's. A one-year limited warranty is stated.

Now, the honest limits. The listing gives its dimensions twice, and the two sets disagree, so I am giving you neither. Reach also stops at eighteen and five-sixteenths inches, short of number three.

Its place in the lineup is the specialist for moving around. Next to number three, it is twenty-eight pounds lighter.

Buy it if you carry your jack. Do not buy it for a shop where it works all day.

##VILLAIN
Before the top three, the promise I made. The Blackhawk B6350 Fast Lift Service Jack.

Automoblog names it its best car floor jack and calls it the most powerful it tested. SlashGear's brand ranking puts Blackhawk first. On paper it is clever: three and a half tons, fast lift, steel build.

Now read that outlet's own cons. Heavy, with a high minimum clearance. Its table lists eighty-five pounds and a five and a half inch start. The listing prints no starting height, and its spec table says maximum lifting height four inches, on a jack that lifts to twenty-two. Picture sliding it at a car with five inches of clearance.

A floor jack that can't get under the car is a heavy doorstop with extra steps.

If your car sits low, buy number one. Buy the Blackhawk only for a truck or tall SUV, or don't buy it.

##P3
Number three, the Yellow Jacket AS84009 4-Ton Low Profile Floor Jack.

This is for a truck, van or tall SUV that needs more capacity and more reach.

Pro Tool Reviews makes the trade plain: higher capacity means slower and heavier, which is why pro jacks use two pumps. The listing states four tons, an eight thousand eight hundred pound capacity, alloy steel, dual pumps, and a lifting range of four and five-sixteenths to nineteen and seven-eighths inches. The spec table agrees at four point three one. The maker claims that reach beats most jacks of the same capacity. It has an overload valve, a detachable handle and a one-year warranty.

Now, the honest limits. It lists eighty-seven pounds. Hold that number, because number two is lighter. It also starts about a third of an inch higher than number four. The Drive notes the same trade on its own Best Overall four ton Big Red: it starts taller than most, and quality control issues are somewhat common. That is a different jack from this one, and I am not blaming this one for it.

Its place in the lineup is the heavy-duty pick, with the most capacity here.

Buy it for a truck or van. Do not buy it for a lowered car, or if you lift the jack often.

##P2
Number two, the SATA 3-Ton Heavy-Duty Steel Ultra-Low Profile Floor Jack.

This is for someone who wants a pro-grade steel jack and has a low car.

The listing states three tons, steel construction, seventy pounds, and a dual piston rapid pump. The maker says it cuts handle effort by forty percent. It also says every unit is tested to one and a half times its rating, and that this exceeds ANSI and ASME requirements. Again, that is the maker's claim. It has an overload valve and a slow release for controlled lowering. Number three weighs eighty-seven pounds, while this weighs seventy and still reaches nineteen and one-eighth inches.

So why isn't it number one? Look at the starting height. The title says two and eleven-sixteenths. The spec table says two point one three. The bullet says three and three-quarters. Three numbers, one page. One printed number is data. Three is a rumor, so I am giving you none.

Now, the honest limits. You must measure it yourself before trusting any of them, and the listing gives no clear warranty term.

Its place in the lineup is the pro pick for a low car, if the lowest figure holds.

Buy it if you are willing to measure on arrival. Do not buy it if your car has zero room to spare.

##P1
Number one, the Big Red AT825051UR 2.5-Ton Low Profile Hydraulic Trolley Jack.

This is for most people with a normal car in a home garage.

Now back to five point one. This jack states a lifting range of three and one-third to fifteen inches in the bullet, and the spec table agrees at eight point four centimeters. That is nearly two inches lower than number six, and over two inches below the Blackhawk. It weighs twenty-seven point seven pounds, the lightest weight any pick lists. Capacity is two and a half tons, and Pro Tool Reviews says most pros get by with that. It has a safety bypass, an overload valve and large steel casters with swivel casters.

Both The Drive and Automoblog recommend other jacks in the Big Red line. Those are different models, and they report quality control problems and quick lowering on theirs. Treat that as a reason to check yours on day one.

Now, the honest limits. Fifteen inches of reach is short. Pro Tool Reviews warns that a jack topping out at fourteen or fifteen can't fully lift a truck on twenty-inch wheels. The listing states no warranty term.

It is number one because it prints the lowest starting height that agrees with itself, it is the lightest pick that lists a weight, and it fits the car most people actually own. Buy it for a car. Do not buy it for a truck.

That's why it's number one.

##FREE
Here is the free habit, and it works with every jack here. Lift, then set a jack stand, then lower the car onto it. Do not put any part of you under a car held only by a jack.

Family Handyman says never depend solely on the hydraulic system to hold a vehicle. The Drive says use stands every time, because small components can give out at any moment. DNA Motoring and Blackhawk both write it into their own bullets.

The Drive adds one more: leave room for suspension travel. Measure from the floor to your lift point first.

That habit costs nothing, and it decides whether the jack is the only thing that fails.
