##HOOK
Same machine. Two testers. Opposite answers. Reader's Digest put an ultrasonic cleaner on a hair pick caked with oils and creams, using only water, and the buildup came off. AOL Shopping ran the same model on a makeup brush smeared with lotion, added dish soap, and the lotion stayed.

Now, you are about to trust your wedding ring or your glasses to one of these. And here is the honest part: I could not find anyone who ran two machines through the same dirt.

So this list goes from a twelve ounce pod to a six liter heated tank. I'll show you what I think explains that argument, why kilohertz is the wrong number to shop by, and which famous name I'd skip. Then there's a free list of what must never go in the tank. All the product links are in the description.

##P6
Number six, the KUNPHY 46kHz 12-ounce Ultrasonic Jewelry Cleaner.

This is for one person with a few rings and very little counter space.

BestReviews named a twelve ounce KUNPHY its Best Portable Cleaner and called it easy to operate. The dimensions it printed differ from this listing, so treat that as a verdict on the family. You get one button and one three-minute cycle, so there is nothing to set. Hold that three minutes, because number one gives you five different cycles.

The maker claims forty-six kilohertz and under forty-five decibels, which would mean a quiet hum in a shared room. The tank is 304 stainless steel, and rubber pads keep it from walking across the counter. It is the only pick here that lists cleaning solution in the box, fifty milliliters of it. It weighs about one and a quarter pounds and runs about seven inches long, so it fits in a drawer or a suitcase.

Now, the honest limits. The body is under two inches wide on the outside, so measure your largest frames before you count on it for glasses. Some users doubt its durability, according to that same roundup. The listing gives no watt rating, no heater and no warranty, so greasy jobs are out.

Its place in the lineup is the entry point. Buy it for rings on a shelf, and skip it if you clean for a whole household.

##P5
Number five, the Ukoke UUC06S 42kHz 600ml Ultrasonic Cleaner.

This is for the household that wants a real tank and a real timer in the lower tier of this list.

Reader's Digest made it their value pick, running black plastic cologne caps through the longest preset with a little dish soap until they came out glossy. You get a six hundred milliliter stainless tank and five timer cycles. The listing says ninety to six hundred seconds, while that test printed four hundred eighty as the top preset, plus a degas setting.

Now, the myth. Shoppers compare kilohertz, and listings shout forty-six, forty-eight, even fifty. Stuller's bench jeweler guide says most ultrasonic cleaners run between thirty-five and forty-five. This listing says forty-two, and that test printed forty. Their top two picks sit right there, and so do the two heated tanks on this list. I found no test showing a few kilohertz changes the result. You are buying heat, time and tank size, not a bigger number.

Now, the honest limits. That same test rated it less durable than their other picks, and the listing shows no wattage and no warranty. It also has no heater, so stubborn grease stays a scrubbing job.

Its place in the lineup is the value pick, a step up in tank size from number six. Buy it for jewelry and glasses, and skip it if you expect years of daily use.

##P4

This is for the tinkerer with greasy small parts, old tools or a drawer of grimy hardware.

Here is where the hook starts to resolve. Stuller's guide says heat makes most cleaning more effective, and that it matters most for grease and oil. This Creworks sets anywhere from sixty-eight to one hundred seventy-six, so it covers that entire window.

The listing gives sixty watts of ultrasonic power at forty kilohertz, a one hundred watt heater and a timer up to thirty minutes. Both the tank and the basket are 304 stainless. Now, the honest limits. No outlet I could find tested this model, so everything here is the maker's claim. The listing also says it removes rust and tarnish, while Magnasonic's own listing says its machine does not remove tarnish. Treat that claim with suspicion, because tarnish is a chemical change, not loose dirt. No warranty appears on this listing either.

Its place in the lineup is the heated specialist. Buy it for grease, and skip it if you only clean a ring.

##FREE
Here is the free upgrade, and it works with every machine on this list. The enemy is not dirt, it is what you drop in the tank.

GIA, the Gemological Institute of America, says pearls, coral, amber and shell cameos should not go in. The same goes for emeralds and opals, and for any stone with filled fractures. If you are unsure about a gem, keep it out.

Then fix the water. iUltrasonic, a cleaner seller, says tap water alone significantly reduces cleaning, and that dish soap works but slower. Degas every fresh fill, because trapped air blocks the bubbles, and never run it underfilled. For coated lenses, ask your optician, since I found no ruling.

That list costs nothing, and it decides whether you clean your ring or crack it.

##TAIL
Before you pay, ask one question: is your dirt grit or grease? Grit comes off a ring in plain water, and grease wants heat. No kilohertz number on a product page tells you which machine you need.

All seven links are in the description, including the iSonic D2840. Check the model number, because VisiClean, InvisiClean and VEVOR all sell similar machines under similar names. Measure your largest item against the basket, not the tank, and remember that only the Creworks and the VEVOR have heaters.
