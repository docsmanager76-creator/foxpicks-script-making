##HOOK
One phone thermal camera prints two hundred forty by one hundred eighty on its box. Its own fine print says the sensor is eighty by sixty. That is four thousand eight hundred real temperature points, sold under a number that suggests forty three thousand. TechRadar's Mark Pickavance wrote that most marketing avoids mentioning that sensor. PCWorld tested another camera and wrote that the upscaling adds no real sensor detail. And it is not one brand. Almost every camera that plugs into a phone sells the stretched number. So I ranked six of them by the detector, not the sticker. I will show you the one box that prints its real pixel count, the one that wins for most people, and the famous name that is today's villain. Plus a free habit that fixes bad readings. All the product links are in the description.

##P6
Number six, the Thermal Master P1 thermal camera for USB-C iPhone and Android.

This is for the person who needs thermal now and then, and wants it in a pocket, not in a toolbox.

The real detector is one sixty by one twenty, so nineteen thousand two hundred true temperature points. The box says three twenty by two forty. TechRadar wrote that the bigger number is extrapolated, which means software stretching the same data. Sensitivity is forty millikelvin, so a cold draft along a window frame still shows up. It reads from minus four to eleven hundred twelve degrees, and the maker rates accuracy at plus or minus three point six degrees. You get fifteen times digital zoom, high and low alarms, an isotherm mode, and emissivity settings. It weighs six tenths of an ounce and has no battery of its own. It pulls just zero point three two watts from the phone, and the maker claims five to eight hours. Hold that number, because a famous camera a tier above it runs on its own battery.

Now, the honest limits. TechRadar found the plastic case less durable than metal rivals, and there is no tripod. The lens is fixed focus. On an iPhone it needs USB-C, because the Lightning adapter is sold separately.

Its place in the lineup is the entry pick.

Buy it for occasional drafts and warm breakers. Skip it if you repair circuit boards.

##P5

The range runs from minus four to one thousand twenty two degrees, and TOPDON rates accuracy at plus or minus two degrees Celsius or two percent. You can measure up to nine spots, lines or areas at once, in nine color palettes. It weighs about an ounce, with a nineteen point seven inch cable in the box. Now, the honest limits. It works with Android only, through an OTG port. TOPDON says to confirm with support that your phone runs the sharpening mode.

Its place in the lineup is the value pick.

Buy it for an Android phone and house work. Skip it if your family uses iPhones.

##FREE
Here is the free upgrade, and it works on every camera on this list.

Shiny metal lies to thermal cameras. PCWorld found that bare metal reflections distort readings, because polished metal gives off little heat of its own and mirrors the room instead. So before you trust a number on a pipe, a busbar or a frying pan, stick a strip of matte masking tape on it. Give the tape a minute to match the surface, then read the tape, not the metal. Then set emissivity in the app for the material you are reading. And remember, thermal does not see through wood, concrete, water or glass. It reads the surface facing it.

That habit costs nothing, and it decides whether you trust a reading or chase a ghost.

##TAIL
Before you buy, ask one question: what is the real detector resolution? Not the super resolution, the sensor. Most product pages bury it in a bullet, and a few never print it at all.

All the product links are in the description. On each listing, check your phone's port, and whether a Lightning adapter is in the box. Two picks, the TOPDON TC001 and the Thermal Master P4, will not work on an iPhone. The FLIR ONE Pro comes in separate Android and iPhone versions, so buy the right one.
