## Script

### HOOK
Bob Vila's editors hands-on tested a magnetic angle gauge and printed its accuracy as plus or minus one tenth of a degree. The maker's own page for that same model says plus or minus two tenths. Same gauge, twice the error.

Then there is the digital trick. One listing in this category shows a display that steps in twentieths of a degree, while its own spec sheet admits an error of two tenths. The decimals are four times sharper than the accuracy.

I ranked six gauges for metalwork and checked every number against the listing I'm linking. You get the countdown, the myth about decimals, the one I'd skip, and a free habit that tightens any of them. All the product links are in the description.


### P6
Number six, the Johnson Level 700 Magnetic Angle Locator.

This is for a fabricator who wants a battery-free angle check in the pocket.

I found no independent test of this one, so this entry runs on the listing and the maker's claims. The listing gives a scale of zero to 90 degrees in all four quadrants, printed under an acrylic lens. An extra-strong ceramic magnet clings to ferrous metal, and a V-groove edge sits on pipe and conduit. A printed scale has no battery to die mid-tack and no screen to flatter the reading.

Now a number to hold. A tenth of a degree on a twelve inch leg is about two hundredths of an inch. That is my arithmetic, not a listing claim. Hold that figure, because number one earns it only in a narrow window.

Now, the honest limits. The listing states no accuracy figure at all, so you cannot know what the scale is worth. That hurts on a fit-up that has to close tight. It does not matter when you are checking pipe slope or a rough bevel.

Its place in the lineup is the entry pick, and the ownership cost is nothing, since there is no battery to replace.

Buy it for rough checks. Do not buy it if your drawing gives tolerances in tenths.


### P5
Number five, the GemRed DAG-V1 Digital Angle Gauge.

This is for a shop that wants a digital readout in a metal frame, as a second gauge on the bench.

I found no outlet test of this exact model. Bob Vila and Woodsmith recommend other GemRed tools, and those verdicts do not carry over. The listing says the framework is aluminum alloy instead of plastic, with a magnetic flat base. It reads zero to 360 degrees as four 90 degree quadrants, in degrees only, with a true level mode and a relative angle mode. The reversible backlit screen reads upside down under a rail.

Now the myth this category runs on. The listing gives a resolution of 0.1 degree and an accuracy of plus or minus 0.2. Those are different things. Resolution is the smallest step the screen can show. Accuracy is how far that step can be wrong. A screen can show a hundredth and still be off by a fifth. On a twelve inch leg, two tenths is about four hundredths of an inch. You're buying a spec sheet, not a measurement.

Now, the honest limits. It reads degrees only, with no percent grade and no inches per foot. That hurts if you work from slope charts. It does not matter for bevels and miters.

Its place in the lineup is the budget tier. The listing claims about 50 hours on one AAA battery, which is the maker's claim.

Buy it as a second gauge. Do not buy it as your only one for tight work.


### P4
Number four, the S&F Stead & Fast Digital Angle Gauge Magnetic Angle Cube.

This is for someone who sets machine tilt and bench alignment and wants a stated repeatability.

No outlet I found tests this one, so this is spec-decode again. The listing claims plus or minus 0.2 degrees accuracy, 0.1 resolution, and repeatability of plus or minus 0.1. That last number is rare. Repeatability is how closely the gauge agrees with itself when you set it down twice, and for fit-up that matters nearly as much as being right. The listing gives a self-inverting backlit display, hold and zero buttons, and absolute and relative modes. The body is aluminum, and the magnetic base is described for ferrous surfaces, machine tilt, workbench alignment and pipe slope.

Next to number five, you get a stated repeatability. Next to number three, you give up the alert and the weather rating.

Now, the honest limits. The listing states no water or dust rating, and it takes two AAA batteries, so keep spares. That hurts in a wet shop. It does not matter at a dry bench.

Its place in the lineup is the mid tier. I found no warranty term in the listing bullets.

Buy it for machine tilt and bench alignment. Do not buy it if you need a dust and water rating on paper.


### VILLAIN
Before the top three, the promise I made. The GemRed 82305 200-millimeter Digital Angle Finder Protractor.

Bob Vila names the 200-millimeter GemRed Best for Beginners, and Woodsmith makes a GemRed its runner up, praising the locking function. On paper it is clever: a stainless steel ruler and protractor in one that locks any angle you read.

Now read the limit. Both outlets say it scratches easily. The listing gives accuracy of plus or minus 0.3 degrees, the loosest figure on this list, and never mentions a magnet. On a twelve inch leg, three tenths is about a sixteenth of an inch. Picture it sliding off a steel frame.

A gauge that won't stay on steel is a ruler with extra steps.

For a bench gauge, buy number five. Buy this one only for flat layout, or don't buy it.


### P3
Number three, the Klein Tools 935DAA Digital Angle Gauge with Angle Alert.

This is for a fitter who sets an angle, then needs both hands for the work.

I found no independent test of this model, so this entry runs on the listing and Klein's own catalog. Klein's catalog states plus or minus 0.1 degree at zero to one, 89 to 91 and 179 to 180, and plus or minus 0.2 everywhere else. The listing gives LEDs and audio that show how close you are to a target angle, so you watch for green instead of reading digits. Rare-earth magnets and V-groove edges hold it to flat steel and pipe. The listing gives an IP54 rating against dust and water, which suits a shop full of grinding dust.

So why isn't it number one? It adds an alert, not accuracy. Klein's catalog gives number one the same stated numbers.

Now, the honest limits. The alert helps you hit an angle, but it cannot make the gauge more accurate than its spec. That hurts if you bought it for precision. It does not matter if you bought it for speed.

Its place in the lineup is the step-up Klein. I found no warranty term in the listing bullets.

Buy it for hands-free setups. Do not buy it expecting tighter accuracy than number one.


### P2
Number two, the Wixey WR300 Digital Angle Gauge.

This is for someone who sets saw blades and machine tables as well as bending metal.

Bob Vila hands-on tested it and named it Best for Saw Blades, praising the backlit screen and magnetic base. Woodsmith tested a Wixey alongside the Beall Tilt Box and the iGaging Angle Cube. All three held to within 0.1 degree, checked by cutting 45, 30 and 22.5 degree angles that closed without gaps. Woodsmith did not say which Wixey model it used.

Now the collision from the hook. Bob Vila prints plus or minus 0.1 for this gauge. The listing gives 0.1 degree resolution and plus or minus 0.2 accuracy, and I am using the maker's number. The listing also gives a zero function, so you can measure relative to a surface that is not level.

So why isn't it number one? The listing states plus or minus 0.2 at every angle and mentions no V-groove for pipe. Number one adds a tighter band near flat and vertical, and a groove that seats on tube.

Now, the honest limits. Sources disagree on its battery, one AAA from Bob Vila and a watch-style cell in Woodsmith's older test, so check the listing before you buy spares. That matters if you work far from a store. It does not matter at a home bench.

Its place in the lineup is the proven pick. I found no warranty term in the listing bullets.

Buy it for saw tables and machine setups. Do not buy it for pipe and tube.


### P1
Number one, the Klein Tools 935DAG Digital Angle Gauge and Level.

This is for most metalworkers, with pipe, tube, bending and fit-up in one pocket tool.

Woodsmith names the Klein its Best Overall angle finder, with strong earth magnets in the base and edges, and notes you cannot use it lying flat. It is the only tool here an outlet crowned best overall.

The listing gives a strong magnetic base for ferromagnetic surfaces like pipe, conduit and saw blades. V-groove edges seat it on conduit and pipe for bending and alignment. The zero to 180 range is there, the listing says, for accounting for springback when bending conduit. A reverse-contrast display rotates when it is upside down.

Remember the tenth of a degree from number six. Here is where it lives. Klein's catalog states plus or minus 0.1 only near zero, 90 and 180 degrees. At a 45 degree bevel it is plus or minus 0.2, the same stated error as number five.

Now, the honest limits. The listing gives the housing as ABS plastic, not metal, and Woodsmith says it will not work lying flat. That hurts on a rough shop floor. It does not matter on a clean bench.

Its place in the lineup is the flagship. I found no warranty term in the listing bullets.

Buy it for pipe, tube and bends. Do not buy it for aluminum work, since magnets will not hold.

Because the cardinal angles are where most fit-up lives, and no other pick seats on tube. That's why it's number one.


### FREE
Now the free upgrade. The surface you set it on lies. A shop table is rarely level, and a steel sliver under the magnet tilts the reading. So wipe the base, set the gauge on a reference surface, press zero, and measure relative to that. Then set it down twice. If two placements disagree, wipe the base and try again. A reading you can repeat beats a digit you cannot. Wixey's own listing says zeroing works even on saws that are not perfectly leveled. That habit costs nothing, and it decides whether you cut once or cut twice.


### TAIL
Before you pay, ask one question. Is that number accuracy or resolution? The screen shows you the step. Only the spec sheet shows you the error, and most listings print only the flattering one.

The Klein gauges turn up at hardware and welding supply shops as well as online, so you can hold one first. Check each listing for a plus or minus figure, not just decimals. A magnet only grabs ferromagnetic metal, so most aluminum and many stainless grades will not hold it.
