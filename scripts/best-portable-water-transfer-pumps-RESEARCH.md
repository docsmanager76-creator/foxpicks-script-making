## STEP 0 — BUYER DECISION CHECKLIST
1. Power source: 120 V cord / tool battery platform / gas
2. Rated flow, and the condition it is rated at
3. Flow at real height (does the maker publish a curve?)
4. Suction lift (how far below the pump it can pull)
5. Max head (how high it can push)
6. Priming: self-priming / fill the port / submersible (no priming)
7. Dry-run protection (auto shutoff)
8. Ports and hose fit (3/4 in garden hose, NPT, what is in the box)
9. What it may pump: clear water only, solids size, glycol, never fuel
10. Weight and size
11. Gallons per charge / duty limits
12. Warranty

## HOOK — type 1 COLLISION
**Re-led at script time as type 4 CONTROLLED CONTRADICTION**: Wall Fireplace Heaters (hook 1) landed in the ledger mid-write. The script opens on the DeWalt DCE050B listing (10 GPM vs 4.5 GPM at 45 ft, same page), then the Wayne HP pair and the Superior flow pair.
One cast-iron pump's own sales page lists **1/2 HP and 1/6 HP**, and **1,450 GPH and 1,350 GPH**. Its maker's manual says **1,600 GPH at 10 ft**. A plastic submersible's page says **1,800 GPH in the bullets and 1,380 GPH in the spec table**. Three numbers, one pump: which do you trust? Named outlet in the hook: Pro Tool Reviews' service plumber flushing tankless water heaters. No pick brands.
Promises: (1) the number that actually predicts your flow; (2) the drill gadget sold at every hardware store; (3) a free hose trick that adds flow to any pump.

## MYTH — "the flow number on the box is the flow you get"
Paid with maker numbers. Wayne's own chart: 1,600 GPH at 10 ft → 660 GPH at 80 ft (−59%). DeWalt's own listing: 10 GPM → 4.5 GPM at 45 ft of head. So the corded Wayne still moves **11 GPM at 80 ft**, while the cordless flagship moves **4.5 GPM at 45 ft**. Line: *"You're buying flow at your height, not the number on the box."* Plant at #5 (Superior, two flow numbers), pay at #3 (curve) and #2.

## VILLAIN — type B HYPE: Milescraft DrillPump750 (1314) — B00F1ZJG5E
- Steel-man: SlashGear (Jul 2025) argues a drill-pump attachment beats buying "a dedicated tool" for occasional jobs. The Milescraft is the best-known drill pump, stocked by Woodcraft, Grizzly, Ace and Canadian Tire. It is self-priming, uses a garden hose, and has a 2-year warranty.
- Kill shot (maker + listing): the 750 GPH needs a drill running about 2,800 RPM (Milescraft). It must be greased with silicone after every use. It is "not to be used with potable water". Its own listing gives 12 ft of lift while Milescraft's site gives 24 m, and the spec table prints "pumps up to 300 gallons per hour". Owner reports: a 50-gallon heater took over half an hour (about 100 GPH); cordless drills are "pretty much" too weak. A drill-pump buyer measured 2.2–2.6 GPM, but that was a **Black & Decker**, so that number stays off the Milescraft.
- Line: *"A pump that borrows your drill is a drill that stops being a drill."* Redirect: #6 for the same job, with no drill to tie up.
- **Weakness:** no named outlet recommends the Milescraft specifically. SlashGear recommends the drill-pump idea (via Ryobi's attachment). If that is too thin, the fallback is a spec villain: the Wayne PC4's own listing contradicting itself (but the Wayne is a pick, so a VEVOR/TOPWAY clone would take its place).

## FREE UPGRADE — short suction side, long discharge side
Every pump pushes better than it pulls. Put the pump as close to the water as the cord or battery allows. Keep the suction hose short and full of water, and let the discharge hose do the distance. Sources: Wayne manual (foot valve past 10 ft of suction lift or 10 ft of suction line; never run dry); Honda spec page (priming takes 110 s at 16.4 ft of lift); Milwaukee 18 ft lift vs 75 ft head (PTR). Second habit: stay with any pump that has no auto shutoff. Seal damage from running dry is the one failure the manual names.
