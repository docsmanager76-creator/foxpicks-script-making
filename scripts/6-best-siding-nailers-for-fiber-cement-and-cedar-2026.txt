##HOOK
The hardest siding wants the gentlest setting. Fiber cement is rock hard and cedar is soft, so you would expect a tough wall to need a tough nailer. JLC's tester found the opposite. One gun shot the thickest nails and still bent them in fiber cement. Depth control mattered more than power. James Hardie's own install sheet agrees: if you cannot dial the depth, under-drive the nail and seat it by hand. That is why I did not rank these by power. I read Bob Vila, JLC, Pro Tool Reviews, and Family Handyman, then checked James Hardie and the maker spec sheets. One of these is the lightest tested. One shoots three-inch nails. One critics' favorite has a catch you feel by noon. And one free step protects every board. Links are in the description.

##FREE
Here is the free upgrade, and it works with every nailer on this list.

First, test on a scrap of the same siding. Start near ninety-five psi, then step the pressure and the depth wheel down until each head sits snug, not crushed. James Hardie says the head should sit flush, never overdriven.

Second, pick the right nail. Hot-dipped galvanized suits fiber cement. For cedar, the Western Red Cedar Lumber Association and JLC both say stainless steel. JLC says never use electroplated nails on cedar.

Third, test again when the wall changes, because sheathing can change from wall to wall.

Fourth, drive at least one and a quarter inches into solid wood, per Family Handyman.

That is how a nailer's depth wheel does the work the power cannot.

##TAIL
Before you buy, ask what you are nailing.

Every nailer here is air-powered, so you need a compressor and a hose. No cordless siding nailer I found was tested on fiber cement or cedar. The cordless ones are roofing tools that top out at one and three-quarter-inch nails.

All seven links are in the description, including the Freeman PCN65, so you can see its weight and nail range. Check the nail length, the depth adjustment, the firing mode, and the weight.
