##HOOK
A full-size scroll saw has a twenty-inch throat, weighs about as much as a mini fridge, and needs a stand it never leaves. If your shop is a corner of a garage, a spare bedroom, or one end of a workbench, that saw was never built for you. Every saw in this video was.

Two more things. One of these saws doesn't even keep a table — it clips out of its own base and works as a handheld tool. And a free technique, straight from a manufacturer's own manual, that fixes the one setup mistake almost every new scroll saw owner makes.

These are the six best compact scroll saws of 2026, built for a small shop or a shared table. Product links are in the description.

This is the only saw here with a rotary shaft port built into the housing. You can drive rotary accessories — sanding drums, polishing wheels — straight off the same motor instead of buying a second tool. It also ships with a foot switch, letting you start and stop the blade with both hands still on the workpiece.

Other included extras: an LED light, a miter gauge for angled crosscuts, and a 1.25 inch dust port. The motor is a sixth horsepower, drawing six tenths of an amp, with a blade stroke of nine sixteenths of an inch — enough for detail work without overpowering thin material.

Now, the honest limits. Shop Fox doesn't publish a weight figure. No professional outlet in this video tested this exact model either — everything here comes from the listing.

Buy it if the rotary shaft port and two-way tilt matter to your projects. Skip it if you want an outlet-verified pick.

##FREEUPGRADE
Now the free fix, and it works on every saw in this video, corded or clamp-on.

WEN's own instruction manual spells out the test for blade tension. Pluck the blade with a finger. A properly tensioned blade rings out at a high C — that's C6, just over a thousand hertz.

Most new owners skip this and guess instead. Guess too loose and the blade wanders off your line or burns the cut. Guess too tight and you snap blades before you've finished a single pattern.

The manual adds one more note. A brand new blade stretches the first time you tension it, so check the pluck again after your first few cuts, not just once at setup.

No new part, no new tool. Just listen before you cut.
