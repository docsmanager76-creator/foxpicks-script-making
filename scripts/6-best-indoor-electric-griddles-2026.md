##HOOK
Every electric griddle sells you one number: a dial marked in degrees. That is why one pancake burns while another stays pale.

America's Test Kitchen took the temperature of each griddle's surface in five different spots. TechGearLab put an infrared thermometer on four corners and the centre of thirteen griddles and calculated the standard deviation. Two labs, separately, decided a griddle does not have a temperature. The box sells you one anyway.

Two more things will surprise you. The best proof comes from a manufacturer, about its own last model. And there is a griddle two of those labs recommend that the third scored last. I'll name it before the top three.

There is also a pancake test that maps your griddle permanently and costs nothing.

The six best indoor electric griddles of 2026. Product links are in the description.
##P6
Number six, the Presto 07211 Liddle Griddle.

Start here if you cook for one or two, or if you want a second griddle that lives in a drawer instead of on the counter. This is the smallest cooking surface in this video by a wide margin, and everybody treats that as the compromise.

It is not entirely a compromise, and here is why. The reason both labs needed five measuring points is that a big griddle has room to disagree with itself. The corners sit further from the element than the middle does. Shrink the surface and you shrink the argument. A small griddle is closer to being one temperature for a boring physical reason, not because anybody engineered it that way.

Now, the honest limits. It is small. Three pancakes, not eight, and a single rasher run of bacon rather than a family breakfast. And nobody has independently tested this one, so everything I just said about size is geometry, not a lab result.

Buy it for a small kitchen or a second surface. Skip it if you are feeding four people at once.

The smallest surface here. Hold that, because it wins an argument the flagship cannot.
##P5
Number five, the Chefman XL 10 by 20-Inch Electric Griddle.

This is the default, and more people end up with it than any other griddle in this video. Ten by twenty inches of nonstick, a slide-out drip tray, and the whole cooktop goes in the dishwasher once you pull the temperature control off.

And now the myth, because it is printed right on this one. The dial reads two hundred to four hundred and twenty degrees. That looks like a measurement. It is a thermostat, it has one sensor, and it is governing a surface that has many temperatures at once.

Watch what happens when a maker stops pretending. America's Test Kitchen tested a rival whose dial is marked one to five, with no Fahrenheit anywhere on it. Same mechanism underneath. One of them is being honest about what a dial actually is.

The proof I promised is better than that, and it is coming at number one.

Now, the honest limit. Ten inches of depth is narrow, and you feel it the moment you turn a row of pancakes and clip the one behind. Reviewed put this griddle through their test and it came out without an award, which is not a failure but is not an endorsement either.

Buy it if you want the most surface for the least outlay. Skip it if you cook deep food in batches.

Now, the honest limit, and it is the most useful thing I can tell you. I cannot tell you how evenly it heats. Nobody has published a heat map of it, and the listing does not publish a temperature range at all, which means the dial on this one is even less of a measurement than the dial on number five. You are buying surface area and a coating, and finding out the rest in your own kitchen.

Buy it for the widest surface at this tier. Skip it if you want somebody else to have checked it first.

##VILLAIN
Before the top three, the griddle I promised.

It is the Zojirushi EA-DCC10 Gourmet Sizzler.

Steel-man it, because two labs are on its side. America's Test Kitchen recommended it, measured the largest cooking surface in their field, and said pancakes cooked evenly on it. Reviewed made it their best griddle with a lid.

Then read the third lab. TechGearLab ran thirteen griddles and this one finished last, forty nine out of a hundred, with three out of ten for grease management. Their words: no grease trap or way of diverting grease from food, leaving our bacon soggy. And a chemical smell that persists even after several uses and permeates food.

The fair part: America's Test Kitchen noticed the same missing grease tray and wrote it up as a footnote. Same fact, opposite weight.

A part nobody misses until they cook bacon is still a missing part. Number two drains grease into a removable tray.
##P3
Number three, the Presto 07061 22-Inch Electric Ceramic Griddle.

Twenty two inches of PFAS-free ceramic with a textured surface, a slide-out drip tray, and a heavy cast aluminium base that is fully immersible once the heat control is off.

The feature people underrate is the handles. They come off. Open two latch clips and the griddle fits inside a standard eighteen inch cabinet. A twenty two inch griddle that does not fit your cupboard ends up living in the garage, and a griddle in the garage is a griddle you never use again.

Now, the honest limit, and it is the whole reason this video exists. TechGearLab made this their best buy, seventy nine out of a hundred. Reviewed tested the same griddle and wrote that consistency proved an issue. Two labs, one griddle, opposite conclusions. That is not a reason to avoid it. It is the reason the free thing at the end of this video exists.

Hold on to cast aluminium, as well. It heats fast and it lifts light, and it has less mass than it looks like it has.

So why isn't it number one? Because two labs could not agree on it, and I am not going to pretend they did.
##P2
Number two, the Hamilton Beach Professional 38560 Cast Iron Electric Griddle and Indoor Grill.

This one is the answer to the whole video.

If the problem is that one sensor is governing a surface with many temperatures, there are only two ways out. Better electronics, or more metal. This is more metal.

Ten by sixteen inches of preseasoned cast iron, adjustable up to four hundred and fifty degrees, with grease pushed through a drain into a removable dishwasher safe tray, and a cooktop that lifts off the base to wipe down.

The listing makes the claim directly, and I will keep it as the maker's claim: cast iron holds heat better than aluminium. It is also just physics. Thermal mass is what stops a cold burger from crashing the surface temperature, and that crash is what a thermostat is always chasing and never catching.

That is the cast aluminium callback from number three. That base heats fast. This one holds.

Now, the honest limits, and there are three. Cast iron is heavy. Mass cuts both ways, so it is slower to preheat than everything below it. And ten by sixteen is a smaller surface than the twenty two inch picks, so you are trading area for stability.

Which is number six's argument arriving at the top of the list. The difference is that this one gets there with metal instead of by accident.

And nobody has independently tested this model either.

Buy it if you cook meat. Skip it if you are feeding eight people pancakes.

Buy this if you cook on a griddle more than once a week. That's why it's number one.
##FREEUPGRADE
Now the free thing, and it works on the griddle already in your cupboard.

Both labs mapped their griddles before they judged them. Five points for one, four corners and the centre for the other. America's Test Kitchen took theirs at five minute intervals, because the surface keeps moving while you cook. You can do the same thing with batter.

Fill the entire surface with identical pancakes, poured within a few seconds of each other. Cook one round, then photograph it.

The pale ones are your cold zone. It is usually the corners that run cool, and America's Test Kitchen found hot spots near the power connection on one griddle.

From then on the bacon goes where it runs hot and the pancakes where it runs even. One batch of batter, and you never have to trust the dial again.
